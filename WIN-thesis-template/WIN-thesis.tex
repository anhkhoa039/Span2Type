\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch-Introduction}

Named Entity Recognition (NER) is a foundational task in natural language processing (NLP), underpinning a wide range of downstream applications including information extraction, knowledge graph construction, question answering, biomedical text mining, and document understanding. The objective of NER is to locate mentions of named entities in text and classify them into predefined semantic categories such as \textsc{Person}, \textsc{Organization}, or \textsc{Location}. Over the past decade, advances in deep learning and pretrained language models have pushed NER performance to near-human levels on standard benchmarks such as CoNLL-2003~\cite{tjong-kim-sang-de-meulder-2003-introduction} and OntoNotes~\cite{pradhan-etal-2013-towards}.

Despite this progress, virtually all high-performing NER systems operate under a \emph{closed-world assumption}: the set of entity types is fixed and known in advance, and models are trained on large amounts of manually annotated data conforming to that type inventory. This assumption, while convenient for benchmark evaluation, creates a fundamental barrier to deploying NER in practice. In real-world settings, new domains constantly emerge---scientific subfields, legal jurisdictions, industrial verticals, social media communities---each with its own vocabulary of relevant entities that may bear little resemblance to the coarse types defined in general-domain corpora. Annotating sufficient labeled data for every new domain is expensive, slow, and often requires specialized expert knowledge. Furthermore, even within a single domain, entity categories are not static: terminology evolves, new entity types emerge, and the granularity of the desired ontology varies by application.

These limitations motivate a shift toward \emph{open-world NER}: a paradigm in which the system receives an unannotated corpus and is expected to (1)~identify entity mention spans, (2)~group those spans into coherent semantic categories without being told how many categories exist or what they are called, and (3)~produce human-readable names for the discovered categories. The third requirement---\emph{automatic entity type naming}---is often treated as an afterthought in existing work, yet it is critical for practical deployment. Clusters of entity mentions without interpretable labels cannot be validated by domain experts, integrated into knowledge bases, or used as supervision signals for downstream tasks. Naming is therefore not merely a cosmetic concern; it is what transforms unsupervised cluster assignments into a usable, inspectable entity type ontology.

This thesis presents \textbf{Span2Type}, an improved open-world NER pipeline built on top of the OWNER framework~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner}, with three targeted enhancements. Span2Type operates in three stages. First, the mention detection module from OWNER---which uses a single BIO sequence labeling head---is replaced with the Entity Detection module from the Divide-and-Transfer (DTrans) paradigm~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}. The DTrans module uses BERT with three complementary tagging schemes applied simultaneously: standard BIO tagging for global span context, Start-End (SE) binary classifiers for explicit boundary detection, and Tie-Break (TB) classifiers that model whether adjacent tokens belong to the same span. Training all three views jointly with a Mean Teaching strategy (exponential moving average) improves domain transfer robustness, since entity span boundaries are more stable across domains than entity types. Second, the entity typing module encodes each detected span using the fill-in-the-blank template \emph{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}.''}\ and extracts the \texttt{[MASK]}-position hidden state from a BERT-based encoder. This thesis introduces a soft prompt variant of this stage: a small number of trainable soft token embeddings are prepended to the template input while the base encoder is kept frozen, so that only the prompt parameters (not the full model) are updated during triplet margin training. The resulting embeddings are clustered with automatic $k$ selection via the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Third, the cluster naming module---which addresses the interpretability gap by generating human-readable type labels from cluster evidence---selects a diverse, representative set of exemplar sentences for each cluster using Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) and applies either a masked language model or a locally hosted large language model (LLM) to generate a concise, human-readable type name by aggregating predictions over the selected exemplars.

Span2Type requires no predefined type inventory and no target-domain annotations. The mention detector is trained on a source-domain corpus (CoNLL-2003 or Pile-NER~\cite{zhou-etal-2024-universalner}) and transferred to new domains without retraining. Experiments on CrossNER spanning five target domains demonstrate that replacing OWNER's BIO-only mention detector with the DTrans multi-view entity detector improves span recall across domains, and that MMR-based exemplar selection produces more specific and stable cluster names compared to OWNER's original naming procedure.

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We integrate the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans} into the OWNER open-world NER pipeline, replacing its single BIO mention detector with a joint BIO + Start-End + Tie-Break decoder that yields higher span recall in cross-domain settings.
    \item We propose an automatic cluster naming procedure that uses Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} to select a diverse set of exemplar sentences per cluster before querying an MLM or LLM, improving naming quality and stability over the centroid-nearest baseline used in the original OWNER system.
    \item We provide a systematic empirical evaluation on CrossNER across five target domains, comparing the improved pipeline against the original OWNER baseline and reporting entity detection F1, entity typing AMI, and cluster naming quality via BERTScore.
\end{itemize}


\section{Motivation} \label{sec:Challenges}

The development of Span2Type is driven by three concrete challenges that arise when attempting to deploy NER systems beyond well-curated benchmark settings.

\paragraph{Challenge 1: Robust span detection across domains without target supervision.}
The first stage of any open-world NER pipeline is detecting entity mention spans in the target corpus without access to target-domain labels. The standard approach, used in OWNER, is to train a BIO sequence tagger on a labeled source domain (e.g., CoNLL-2003) and apply it directly to the target domain. While effective in closely related domains, single-view BIO taggers struggle when target domain text differs substantially in structure or vocabulary from the source. Entity boundary signals---such as whether a token starts a new mention or is connected to the preceding token---can be captured from multiple complementary perspectives. A multi-view detector that jointly trains BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break decoders can exploit these different signals simultaneously, producing span candidates that are more robust to domain shift. This thesis addresses this challenge by replacing OWNER's BIO-only mention detector with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}.

\paragraph{Challenge 2: Unknown and evolving entity type inventories.}
Even in domains where NER data exist, the type inventory may be incomplete or misaligned with the application's needs. Standard NER benchmarks use small, coarse-grained label sets (typically four to eighteen types). Many real-world applications require finer granularity---distinguishing, for example, between \textsc{Drug}, \textsc{Chemical}, and \textsc{Disease} rather than a single \textsc{Medical} type---or operate on entity categories that have no counterpart in existing ontologies, such as entities specific to proprietary product catalogs or newly coined technical terms. Purely supervised methods cannot discover new types; they can only classify entities into the types they were trained on. Open-world NER, by contrast, makes no assumptions about the number or nature of entity types and can surface latent categories that would be missed by any closed-world system.

\paragraph{Challenge 3: The interpretability gap in unsupervised entity clustering.}
Existing unsupervised entity typing methods typically produce cluster assignments without associated type names. The output of such a system is a mapping from entity spans to cluster identifiers (e.g., \textit{Cluster 3}, \textit{Cluster 7}), which carries no semantic content. Practitioners who wish to use these assignments must manually inspect cluster members, guess an appropriate label, and verify that the label is consistent across the cluster---a process that reintroduces the very human effort that unsupervised methods were supposed to eliminate. Automatic type naming bridges this interpretability gap: by generating a concise label such as \textsc{Pharmaceutical Company} or \textsc{Protein Complex} directly from cluster evidence, the system enables human experts to validate the discovered ontology and integrate it into downstream pipelines with minimal manual intervention.

Together, these three challenges motivate the three improvements this thesis makes to the OWNER pipeline. The DTrans multi-view entity detector addresses Challenge~1 by capturing complementary span boundary signals that transfer better across domains. The soft prompt entity typing variant improves entity embedding quality with drastically reduced parameter overhead, producing more type-discriminative representations for clustering. The MMR-based cluster naming module addresses Challenge~3 by producing human-readable type names from diverse cluster evidence rather than leaving practitioners with anonymous cluster identifiers. Challenge~2---the unknown number of entity types---is handled by the BIC-guided $k$ selection already present in OWNER, which this thesis retains unchanged.


\section{Contributions} \label{sec:Overview}

This thesis makes the following specific technical contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-view entity detection for open-world NER.} We replace the BIO-only mention detector in OWNER with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which jointly trains three complementary decoders---BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break---on the source domain. The Mean Teaching strategy (exponential moving average) further stabilizes training, yielding more reliable span candidates when transferred to unseen target domains.

    \item \textbf{Two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning for entity typing.} We introduce a parameter-efficient variant of the entity typing stage that trains in two stages. Stage~1 prepends $N_p$ trainable soft token embeddings to the fill-in-the-blank template and optimizes only these tokens at a high learning rate while the BERT backbone is strictly frozen, allowing rapid convergence without perturbing pre-trained weights. Stage~2 unfreezes the backbone at a drastically lower learning rate ($1\times10^{-5}$) to prevent catastrophic forgetting, while the optimizer's accumulated first- and second-moment statistics for the soft prompt are preserved via \texttt{add\_param\_group()}. This two-stage schedule achieves the parameter efficiency of prompt-only tuning in early epochs and the full expressiveness of fine-tuning in later epochs.

    \item \textbf{MMR-based exemplar selection for automatic cluster naming.} We propose selecting a diverse, centroid-relevant set of exemplar sentences per cluster using Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} before querying either an MLM or a locally hosted LLM for type name generation. Compared to OWNER's original naming procedure, MMR-based selection reduces exemplar redundancy and produces more specific and stable cluster names.

    \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation on CrossNER.} We evaluate the improved pipeline on five CrossNER target domains~\cite{liu-etal-2021-crossner}, reporting span detection F1, entity typing Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI), and cluster naming quality via BERTScore, with ablations that isolate the contribution of each component.
\end{itemize}


\nochapterspacing{Related Work}\label{ch:Work}

\section{Named Entity Recognition}

NER has evolved from supervised sequence labeling with CRFs~\cite{lafferty-etal-2001-crf} and BiLSTM-CRF architectures~\cite{ma-hovy-2016-end} to transformer-based models using BERT~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} and RoBERTa~\cite{liu-etal-2019-roberta}, achieving near-human performance on benchmarks such as CoNLL-2003~\cite{tjong-kim-sang-de-meulder-2003-introduction}. Cross-domain and few-shot methods~\cite{yang-katiyar-2020-simple, ma-etal-2022-label} reduce annotation cost but still require a predefined type inventory and at least a few labeled examples per type.

Open-world NER removes these constraints entirely: the model receives an unannotated corpus and must discover entity types without any predefined ontology. The motivation for this paradigm is reinforced by fine-grained entity typing research: Choi et al.~\cite{choi-etal-2018-ultra} demonstrate that real-world entity categories number in the thousands---far beyond the four to eighteen types defined in standard benchmarks---making any fixed type inventory an approximation at best. Systems such as UniversalNER~\cite{zhou-etal-2024-universalner} and ChatIE~\cite{wei-etal-2023-chatie} use LLM prompting for open-vocabulary extraction, while GLiNER~\cite{zaratiana-etal-2023-gliner} trains a span-level classifier that accepts arbitrary user-specified type names at inference time; however, both families of approaches still require type names to be supplied by the user, a constraint that unsupervised open-world NER removes entirely. Clustering-based approaches instead embed entity mentions and group them by type without any type name input. The OWNER framework~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} is the direct baseline for this thesis: it trains a DeBERTa-v3-based~\cite{he-etal-2023-debertav3} BIO mention detector on a source domain, encodes entities via a fill-in-the-blank BERT template, and clusters embeddings with BIC-guided $k$-means~\cite{macqueen-1967-kmeans, schwarz-1978-bic} to assign unsupervised type labels. This thesis builds on OWNER and improves its span detection and cluster naming components.

\section{Multi-View Span Detection}

Early mention detectors for NER adopt BIO sequence labeling~\cite{ma-hovy-2016-end}, which classifies each token as Beginning, Inside, or Outside an entity. For example, in the sentence \textit{``New York City is home to many banks''}, BIO tagging labels \texttt{New} as B, \texttt{York} and \texttt{City} as I, and all remaining tokens as O. While this suffices for simple proper nouns, multi-token technical terms such as \textit{``convolutional neural network''} or \textit{``support vector machine''} require the tagger to correctly chain three or more consecutive I labels---an error in any one position breaks the span.

Span-based extractors~\cite{zhong-chen-2021-frustratingly} instead enumerate candidate spans and score them directly, offering better handling of overlapping and nested entities---a practical concern in open-world domains where type boundaries are not predefined and entity mentions may share tokens~\cite{li-etal-2020-unified}. Complementary decoding views have been proposed to capture different aspects of entity boundaries: Start-End (SE) classifiers mark the first and last token of each entity independently (so \texttt{New} is tagged as Start and \texttt{City} as End), while Tie-or-Break (TB) classifiers predict for each adjacent token pair whether the two tokens belong to the same span (Tie) or cross a boundary (Break). Compared to a BIO tagger that must propagate state across the full span, SE and TB signals are local: they depend on at most two tokens, making them more stable when surface forms change across domains.

The Divide-and-Transfer (DTrans) paradigm~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans} combines all three views---BIO, SE, and TB---in a single BERT encoder jointly trained with a Mean Teaching strategy (exponential moving average) to stabilize cross-domain transfer. At inference time, the final span set is the \emph{union} of candidates decoded from each view: if BIO misses a span that TB correctly identifies as a contiguous block, the union still captures it. This design produces a candidate pool with higher recall than any single decoder alone. This thesis replaces OWNER’s BIO-only detector with DTrans, directly motivated by its stronger cross-domain span recall.

\paragraph{Why multi-view decoding helps in open-world settings.}
In closed-world NER, the type inventory is fixed, so the tagger can memorize domain-specific surface patterns. In open-world NER, target entity surfaces are unseen during training, making memorization impossible. The SE and TB decoders capture structural properties of entity boundaries---start/end positions and token adjacency---that are more domain-invariant than the sequential context encoded by BIO. This structural generalization is the core reason DTrans outperforms BIO-only detectors when source and target domains diverge.

\section{Fill-in-the-Blank Prompts for Entity Typing}\label{sec:related_template}

\subsection{From Knowledge Probing to Entity Representation}

The fill-in-the-blank (cloze) prompting approach for entity typing originates from the LAMA (Language Model Analysis) benchmark~\cite{petroni-etal-2019-language}, which showed that pretrained masked language models (MLMs) encode factual world knowledge implicitly in their parameters. Given the prompt \textit{``The capital of France is \texttt{[MASK]}''}, BERT predicts \textit{Paris} without any fine-tuning---demonstrating that relational knowledge can be extracted by choosing the right template and reading the \texttt{[MASK]}-position prediction.

Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang-etal-2020-know} extended this idea systematically, showing that the choice of template wording significantly affects the quality of the elicited knowledge. Templates that closely mirror the pretraining data distribution perform best. For entity typing specifically, the template:
\begin{equation*}
    \textit{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}.''}
\end{equation*}
is effective because (i)~the phrase \textit{``X is a \texttt{[MASK]}''} matches the copula-predicate construction common in definitional sentences in BERT’s pretraining corpus (Wikipedia, BookCorpus), and (ii)~repeating the entity surface form after the full sentence provides the model with two complementary signals: the sentence-level context and a direct referential cue about which span to type.

\paragraph{Concrete example.}
Consider the sentence \textit{``BERT was introduced by Google researchers in 2018''} and the detected span \textit{BERT}. The template expands to:
\begin{center}
\texttt{[CLS] BERT was introduced by Google researchers in 2018 BERT is a [MASK] . [SEP]}
\end{center}
BERT (the model) reads this sequence and predicts the token that best completes the blank. Given the sentence context, top predictions include \textit{model}, \textit{system}, \textit{framework}---all semantically appropriate entity type labels. The hidden state at the \texttt{[MASK]} position captures this type-specific prediction distribution and is used as the entity embedding $\mathbf{e}_s$. Two entities of the same type (e.g., \textit{BERT} and \textit{GPT}) will produce similar \texttt{[MASK]} embeddings because both predict similar type tokens; entities of different types (e.g., \textit{BERT} the model vs.\ \textit{Jeff Dean} the person) will predict different tokens and produce dissimilar embeddings. This makes the \texttt{[MASK]}-position representation inherently type-discriminative---a property that BIC-guided $k$-means can exploit for clustering.

\subsection{Why This Template Over Alternatives}

Several template designs are possible. A naive approach would use the span embedding directly (e.g., mean-pooling token hidden states), but this encodes surface-form similarity rather than semantic type: \textit{New York} and \textit{London} would be close (both city names, similar capitalization patterns) but no closer than \textit{New York} and \textit{New Balance} (same first token). The fill-in-the-blank template avoids this by asking the model to \emph{predict a type word}, which activates type-level knowledge from pretraining rather than surface-level lexical similarity.

Alternative templates such as \textit{``\{entity\} is a type of \texttt{[MASK]}''} or \textit{``\{entity\} belongs to the category of \texttt{[MASK]}''} are also valid, but the sentence-contextualized form \textit{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}''} is preferred because it provides the surrounding sentence as context---essential when the entity surface form alone is ambiguous (e.g., \textit{Mercury} could be a planet, a chemical element, or a music band depending on context).

\section{Parameter-Efficient Entity Typing}

Full fine-tuning of the BERT encoder updates all $\sim$110M parameters, which is expensive and prone to overfitting when source-domain training data is limited. Parameter-efficient alternatives prepend a small set of trainable \emph{soft prompt} tokens to the input while keeping the backbone frozen. Prefix-tuning~\cite{li-liang-2021-prefix} and P-tuning~\cite{liu-etal-2022-ptuning} demonstrate that this reduces trainable parameters by orders of magnitude with minimal performance loss, and the Power of Scale result~\cite{lester-etal-2021-power} shows prompt tuning matches full fine-tuning at sufficient model scale. Alternative parameter-efficient approaches include adapter layers~\cite{houlsby-etal-2019-parameter}, which insert small trainable bottleneck modules between transformer layers, and LoRA~\cite{hu-etal-2022-lora}, which reparameterizes weight updates as low-rank matrices; both achieve strong performance but require architectural modifications to the backbone. This thesis adopts soft prompt tuning due to its zero architectural overhead and direct compatibility with the existing fill-in-the-blank entity typing template. This thesis introduces a two-stage soft prompt schedule for entity typing, where the prompt warms up with the backbone frozen before a careful full-model fine-tuning phase.

\paragraph{Intuition for soft prompts.}
A soft prompt token is a trainable vector in the same embedding space as word tokens, but it is not tied to any word in the vocabulary---it is an abstract ``instruction'' that the model learns to interpret. Concretely, prepending $N_p = 5$ soft tokens to the template input is analogous to writing a short task-specific preamble before the sentence: the frozen BERT encoder reads these tokens and adjusts its attention patterns accordingly, without any of its own weights changing. The soft tokens act as a learned signal that nudges the model toward producing more type-discriminative \texttt{[MASK]} representations for the entity typing objective.

\section{Automatic Cluster Naming}

Once entity clusters are formed, assigning human-readable type names is essential for interpretability. Without names, the output of an open-world NER system is a mapping from entity spans to anonymous cluster identifiers (e.g., \textit{Cluster 3}, \textit{Cluster 7}), which carries no semantic content and cannot be used by downstream systems or validated by domain experts.

\paragraph{MLM-based naming.}
The original OWNER approach applies the fill-in-the-blank BERT template to \emph{all} entities in each cluster and selects the most frequently predicted token across all predictions as the cluster name~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner}. For example, if a cluster contains \textit{Einstein}, \textit{Newton}, and \textit{Darwin}, the MLM might predict \textit{physicist}, \textit{mathematician}, and \textit{biologist} respectively; majority voting selects \textit{physicist} as the cluster name (or \textit{scientist} if the template is conditioned on broader context). This approach is fast and requires no external model, but is constrained to single-token predictions.

\paragraph{LLM-based naming.}
LLM-based naming~\cite{brown-etal-2020-language} offers richer, multi-word labels by prompting a generative model with cluster examples, as demonstrated by systems such as GOLLiE~\cite{sainz-etal-2023-gollie}. For the same cluster, an LLM prompt such as \textit{``Given the entities \{Einstein, Newton, Darwin\}, predict the entity type''} might return \textit{``historical scientist''} or \textit{``famous researcher''}---labels that a single \texttt{[MASK]} token cannot express.

\paragraph{Exemplar selection.}
A key challenge in both backends is exemplar selection: which cluster members to include in the prompt. The quality of in-context examples has been shown to substantially affect LLM output in information extraction tasks~\cite{brown-etal-2020-language}: poorly chosen examples introduce noise, while diverse, representative examples elicit more accurate and generalizable predictions. Centroid-nearest selection can produce redundant examples that repeat the same surface form (e.g., selecting only \textit{Einstein}, \textit{Bohr}, and \textit{Heisenberg} for a physics cluster dominated by quantum physicists, missing chemists and biologists also in the cluster), while random sampling may miss the cluster’s dominant type. Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} addresses this by iteratively selecting items that are both relevant to a query (here, the cluster centroid) and dissimilar to already-selected items. Originally proposed for extractive document summarization, MMR has since been applied to in-context example selection and retrieval-augmented generation; this thesis applies it to entity cluster exemplar selection to improve the stability and specificity of both MLM and LLM naming.



\nochapterspacing{Methodology}\label{sec:Method}

\section{System Overview}

Span2Type is a three-stage pipeline for open-world NER that takes an unannotated target-domain corpus as input and produces a set of entity spans, each assigned to a human-readable entity type. The three stages are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Entity Detection}: Given a sentence, detect all entity mention spans using a multi-view span detector trained on a labeled source domain and transferred to the target domain without retraining.
    \item \textbf{Entity Typing}: Encode each detected span using a fill-in-the-blank template and a pretrained masked language model, then cluster the resulting embeddings to assign unsupervised type identifiers. A soft prompt variant replaces full fine-tuning with parameter-efficient prompt tuning.
    \item \textbf{Automatic Cluster Naming}: Assign a concise, human-readable name to each cluster by selecting diverse representative exemplars via Maximal Marginal Relevance and querying either an MLM or a large language model for consensus type predictions.
\end{enumerate}

The pipeline assumes no target-domain labels at any stage. Stages 2 and 3 are applied entirely to the target domain. Stage 1 uses source-domain labels only for training the entity detector. Figure~\ref{fig:span2type_framework} illustrates the overall architecture. Table~\ref{table:notations} summarizes the notation used throughout this chapter.

\section{Stage 1: Multi-View Entity Detection}

\subsection{Motivation}

The original OWNER pipeline uses a DeBERTa-v3-base encoder with a single BIO sequence labeling head to detect entity spans. While BIO tagging is effective when the source and target domains are similar, it captures only one view of span boundary information. An entity mention can be characterized from at least three complementary perspectives: its internal BIO label sequence, the positions of its start and end tokens, and the pairwise ``tied or broken'' relationship between adjacent tokens. Using all three simultaneously produces richer and more transferable span representations.

This thesis replaces OWNER's BIO-only detector with the Entity Detection (ED) module from the Divide-and-Transfer (DTrans) paradigm~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which implements exactly this multi-view strategy.

\subsection{Architecture}

The ED module uses BERT-base-cased~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} as its backbone encoder. Given an input sequence $\mathbf{x}$, the encoder produces contextual representations $\mathbf{H} = (\mathbf{h}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_T) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$. Three independent classification heads are applied to $\mathbf{H}$ in parallel:

\paragraph{BIO decoder.}
A linear classifier maps each token hidden state to a three-class distribution over $\{B, I, O\}$:
\begin{equation}
    p^{\text{BIO}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{bio}}\,\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\text{bio}}),
    \label{eq:bio}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{\text{bio}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times d}$. This head captures the global sequential context of entity spans.

\paragraph{Start-End (SE) decoder.}
Two binary classifiers independently predict whether each token is the start or end of an entity span. The end classifier conditions on the predicted start probability to improve boundary coherence:
\begin{equation}
    p^{\text{start}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{start}}\,\mathbf{h}_t), \qquad
    p^{\text{end}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{end}}\,[\mathbf{h}_t; \hat{p}^{\text{start}}_t]),
    \label{eq:se}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot\,;\,\cdot]$ denotes concatenation and $\hat{p}^{\text{start}}_t$ is the soft start label (one-hot during training, softmax output during inference). This head provides explicit boundary position signals that complement the sequential BIO labels.

\paragraph{Tie-Break (TB) decoder.}
A binary classifier predicts whether each adjacent pair of tokens $(t, t{+}1)$ belongs to the same entity span (\textit{Tie}) or crosses a boundary (\textit{Break}). The hidden representation for the pair is computed as the element-wise sum of the hidden states of both tokens:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}^{\text{pair}}_t = \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{h}_{t+1}, \qquad
    p^{\text{tb}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{tb}}\,\mathbf{h}^{\text{pair}}_t).
    \label{eq:tb}
\end{equation}
This head explicitly models token-level adjacency within spans, capturing information that BIO and SE alone may miss.

\subsection{Training Objective}

The model is trained on the labeled source domain. The total loss is the sum of the cross-entropy losses from all three decoders:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{ED}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BIO}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}},
    \label{eq:ed_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}} = (\mathcal{L}_{\text{start}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{end}}) / 2$.

To stabilize training and improve generalization, a \textbf{Mean Teaching} strategy based on Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is applied. A teacher model $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ maintains a running average of the student model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t = \alpha\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_t,
    \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.995$. The teacher generates soft pseudo-labels on unlabeled data, which are added as an auxiliary consistency loss weighted by $\lambda_w$, encouraging the student to produce stable predictions.

\subsection{Inference}

At inference time, the three decoders independently decode span candidates from their respective predictions. The final entity span set is the union of candidates from all three decoders after applying a confidence threshold $\delta$ to filter low-confidence predictions. Spans from different views that overlap are merged, giving the downstream entity typing stage a high-recall set of candidates.


\section{Stage 2: Entity Typing}

\subsection{Fill-in-the-Blank Entity Representation}

For each detected entity span $s = (i, j)$ in sentence $\mathbf{x}$, the entity typing stage constructs a type-sensitive representation by filling a template. The rationale for this template design---rooted in the LAMA cloze-testing framework~\cite{petroni-etal-2019-language}---is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related_template}. The template used throughout this thesis is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{template}(s, \mathbf{x}) = \texttt{[CLS] } x_1 \cdots x_T \texttt{ } m \texttt{ is a [MASK] . [SEP]},
    \label{eq:template}
\end{equation}
where $m = x_i \cdots x_j$ is the surface form of the entity mention. Note that $m$ is a span already contained within the sentence $x_1 \cdots x_T$; the explicit repetition gives the model a direct cue about which span to type.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Example.}
Given the sentence \textit{``Albert Einstein was born in Germany''} and the detected span $s{=}(\textit{Albert},\,\textit{Einstein})$, the template expands to:\\[4pt]
\texttt{[CLS] Albert Einstein was born in Germany Albert Einstein is a [MASK] . [SEP]}\\[4pt]
BERT reads this sequence and predicts the token that best completes the blank, e.g.\ \textit{physicist} or \textit{scientist}.
The hidden state at the \texttt{[MASK]} position encodes the type-specific meaning of the span and is used as the entity embedding $\mathbf{e}_s$.
\end{quote}

This template is tokenized and passed through a BERT-based encoder. The hidden state at the \texttt{[MASK]} position serves as the entity embedding $\mathbf{e}_s \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}_s = \text{Dropout}(\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[MASK]}}),
    \label{eq:mask_emb}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[MASK]}}$ is the last-layer hidden state at the mask position. Because the template implicitly asks the model to predict the type category of the entity, the \texttt{[MASK]}-position representation is more type-discriminative than a raw span embedding.

\subsection{Training with Batch Triplet Margin Loss}

The entity encoder is fine-tuned on the source-domain training set using a \textbf{Batch Triplet Margin Loss}~\cite{schroff-etal-2015-facenet}. For a batch of $N$ entities with embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}_{i=1}^N$ and source-domain type labels $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^N$, all valid $(anchor, positive, negative)$ triplets are extracted in-batch. A triplet $(i, j, k)$ is valid if $y_i = y_j$ and $y_i \neq y_k$. The loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{triplet}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{(i,j,k) \in \mathcal{T}} \max\!\left(0,\; \|\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j\|_2 - \|\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_k\|_2 + \gamma\right),
    \label{eq:triplet}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of valid triplets and $\gamma = 1.0$ is the margin. This objective pushes embeddings of the same type closer together and embeddings of different types further apart, yielding a metric space where $k$-means clustering recovers semantically coherent entity type groups.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Example.}
Suppose a batch contains three entities from CoNLL-2003: \textit{Obama} (PER), \textit{Biden} (PER), and \textit{Google} (ORG). The triplet $(\textit{Obama},\,\textit{Biden},\,\textit{Google})$ is valid: the anchor \textit{Obama} and the positive \textit{Biden} share the PER label, while the negative \textit{Google} has a different label (ORG). The loss term for this triplet is $\max(0,\;\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{Obama}}-\mathbf{e}_{\text{Biden}}\|_2 - \|\mathbf{e}_{\text{Obama}}-\mathbf{e}_{\text{Google}}\|_2 + 1.0)$. If \textit{Obama} is already closer to \textit{Biden} than to \textit{Google} by a margin of at least 1.0, the term is zero (no update needed). Otherwise, the gradient pushes \textit{Obama} and \textit{Biden} closer and \textit{Obama} and \textit{Google} further apart. After training, all PER embeddings cluster together and all ORG embeddings cluster separately---exactly the structure $k$-means needs to assign cluster identities.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Soft Prompt Variant}

Standard fine-tuning updates all parameters of the BERT encoder, which can be expensive and may overfit when training data is limited. This thesis also proposes a \textbf{soft prompt} variant inspired by P-tuning~\cite{liu-etal-2022-ptuning, lester-etal-2021-power}, where the base encoder is kept frozen and a small set of $N_p$ trainable soft token embeddings $\mathbf{P} = [\mathbf{p}_1; \ldots; \mathbf{p}_{N_p}] \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times d}$ is prepended to the token embedding sequence before being passed to the frozen encoder:
\begin{equation}
    \text{inputs\_embeds} = [\mathbf{P};\; \mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{x}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{(N_p + L) \times d},
    \label{eq:soft_prompt}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{x}}$ are the standard token embeddings of the template-expanded input of length $L$. The attention mask is extended accordingly to cover the prepended soft tokens. The \texttt{[MASK]}-position index is shifted by $N_p$ to account for the prepended tokens. With $N_p = 5$ (default), this reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\sim$110M to just $5 \times 768 = 3{,}840$.

The same batch triplet margin loss (Equation~\ref{eq:triplet}) is used to train the soft prompt parameters.

\paragraph{Two-Stage Prompt-Initialized Fine-Tuning.}
A naive approach would either keep the backbone frozen for the entire run (limiting expressiveness) or fine-tune it from the first epoch at a standard learning rate (risking catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge~\cite{kirkpatrick-etal-2017-overcoming}). This thesis proposes a \textbf{two-stage} schedule controlled by a threshold epoch $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$:

\textbf{Stage~1 — Soft Prompt Warmup} (epochs $1$ to $E_{\text{unfreeze}} - 1$): The PLM is strictly frozen and the AdamW optimizer tracks only $\mathbf{P}$. A high learning rate $\eta_{\text{prompt}}^{(1)} = 3 \times 10^{-3}$ allows the prompt to converge rapidly in the low-dimensional parameter space without perturbing the pre-trained backbone.

\textbf{Stage~2 — Full Fine-Tuning} (epochs $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$ to $E_{\text{total}}$): The PLM is unfrozen and its parameters are added to the optimizer as a new parameter group at a drastically lower learning rate:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\text{PLM}}^{(2)} = 1 \times 10^{-5} \;\ll\; \eta_{\text{prompt}}^{(1)},
    \label{eq:two_stage_lr}
\end{equation}
The large disparity between learning rates is essential: a high PLM learning rate at Stage~2 would overwrite the pre-trained representations, degrading generalization on unseen domains.

Two implementation details are critical for correctness. First, the PLM parameters are added to the existing optimizer via \texttt{add\_param\_group()} rather than reinitializing the full optimizer. This preserves the first- and second-moment estimates ($m_t$, $v_t$) accumulated for $\mathbf{P}$ during Stage~1 — meaningful gradient statistics for the already-trained soft tokens — while the newly added PLM parameters receive fresh zero-initialized moments, which is appropriate since they have not yet been updated. Second, the learning rate scheduler is reset at the $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$ boundary so that $\eta_{\text{PLM}}^{(2)}$ decays from its full value rather than inheriting a mid-schedule multiplier left over from Stage~1.

\subsection{BIC-Guided Cluster Assignment}

After the encoder is trained, all entity spans in the target domain are encoded into embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_s\}$. Since the number of entity types in the target domain is unknown, the system must determine $k$ automatically. This is achieved via \textbf{AutoKmeans}, which searches over a range $[k_{\min}, k_{\max}]$ with step $k_{\text{step}}$ and selects the $k^*$ that minimizes the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

For a fitted $k$-means model~\cite{macqueen-1967-kmeans} with inertia $\mathcal{I}(k)$ (sum of squared distances to cluster centroids) on $n$ samples, the BIC~\cite{schwarz-1978-bic} is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{BIC}(k) = n \cdot \log\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}(k)}{n}\right) + \log(n) \cdot k.
    \label{eq:bic}
\end{equation}
The first term measures fit quality: it decreases as $k$ grows and the clustering better approximates the data. The second term is a complexity penalty that increases with $k$. The optimal number of clusters is:
\begin{equation}
    k^* = \arg\min_{k \in \{k_{\min},\, k_{\min}+k_{\text{step}},\, \ldots,\, k_{\max}\}} \text{BIC}(k).
    \label{eq:k_star}
\end{equation}
GridSearchCV with $k$-means++~\cite{arthur-vassilvitskii-2007-kmeanspp} initialization and 10 random restarts is used for each candidate $k$. The best model (at $k^*$) is then used to assign cluster labels $c_s \in \{1, \ldots, k^*\}$ to every entity span.


\section{Stage 3: Automatic Cluster Naming}

\subsection{MMR-Based Exemplar Selection}

Given a cluster $c$ with entity spans $\mathcal{E}_c$ and their embeddings, the naming module first selects a small set of $K_e$ diverse, centroid-relevant exemplar sentences using \textbf{Maximal Marginal Relevance}~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use}.

Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_c|}\sum_{s \in \mathcal{E}_c} \mathbf{e}_s$ be the cluster centroid. MMR iteratively builds the exemplar set $\mathcal{R}$ by selecting at each step the span that maximizes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MMR}(s) = \lambda \cdot \cos(\mathbf{e}_s, \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) - (1-\lambda) \cdot \max_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \cos(\mathbf{e}_s, \mathbf{e}_r),
    \label{eq:mmr}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ controls the trade-off between centroid relevance (first term) and diversity relative to already-selected exemplars (second term). A value of $\lambda = 0.7$ is used by default, slightly favouring relevance over diversity. The process repeats until $|\mathcal{R}| = K_e$ (default $K_e = 16$). This ensures that the selected exemplars cover the semantic spread of the cluster rather than all clustering near the centroid.

\subsection{MLM-Based Naming}

In the MLM backend, each selected exemplar sentence $x^{(r)}$ containing entity mention $m^{(r)}$ is inserted into a naming template, e.g.:
\begin{equation*}
    \texttt{In this sentence, } m^{(r)} \texttt{ is a [MASK]. Sentence: } x^{(r)}.
\end{equation*}
The template is tokenized and passed through a pretrained BERT MLM. The probability distribution over the vocabulary at the \texttt{[MASK]} position is extracted, and the distributions from all $K_e$ exemplars are averaged:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{p}_v = \frac{1}{K_e} \sum_{r=1}^{K_e} p_v^{(r)}, \quad v \in \mathcal{V}.
    \label{eq:mlm_avg}
\end{equation}
The candidate tokens are filtered to remove sub-word pieces, special tokens, punctuation, and common stopwords. The top-ranked remaining token is selected as the cluster name. This consensus over diverse exemplars produces more stable and specific names than querying a single representative instance.

\subsection{LLM-Based Naming}

In the LLM backend, the $K_e$ MMR-selected entity mentions are collected into a set $\{e_1, \ldots, e_{K_e}\}$ and sent to a locally hosted language model (via Ollama) using the two-turn prompt from the OWNER paper (Fig.~3 in~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner}):

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{System:} \textit{A virtual assistant answers questions from a user based on the provided text.}\\[4pt]
\textbf{User:} \textit{Given the list of entities $\{e_1, \ldots, e_{K_e}\}$, which all belong to the same type, predict the entity type corresponding to all these entities. Respond only with the name of the entity type.}
\end{quote}

The model is queried with temperature~0 for deterministic output. The raw response is post-processed to retain at most three words, strip filler phrases, and normalize to lowercase. Unlike the MLM backend, the LLM can produce multi-word type names (e.g., \textsc{research institute}) that a single \texttt{[MASK]} token cannot express.

The two naming backends are interchangeable within the pipeline; ablation experiments in Chapter~4 compare their naming quality.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\paragraph{Entity Detection.}
The DTrans multi-view entity detector uses \textbf{BERT-base-cased}~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} as the backbone encoder (12 layers, hidden size 768). Training hyperparameters follow~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}: learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, batch size 32, 50 epochs, warmup steps 500, EMA decay $\alpha = 0.995$, consistency weight 100. The maximum sequence length is 128 tokens. Experiments are run with two GPUs.

\paragraph{Entity Typing.}
The entity encoding model uses \textbf{BERT-base-uncased} with the template \emph{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}.''}\ and a batch triplet margin loss ($\gamma = 1.0$). For the standard fine-tuning variant, AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ is used for 30 epochs. For the soft prompt variant, two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning (Section~3.3) is applied over 30 epochs: Stage~1 trains only the $N_p = 5$ soft prompt tokens at $\eta = 3\times10^{-3}$ for 10 epochs with the PLM strictly frozen; Stage~2 unfreezes the PLM and fine-tunes both prompt and backbone at $1\times10^{-5}$ for the remaining 20 epochs, with the learning rate scheduler reset at the boundary. Both stages use a linear decay schedule with no warmup steps.

\paragraph{Clustering.}
AutoKmeans searches $k \in [2, 30]$ with step 2, using $k$-means++~\cite{arthur-vassilvitskii-2007-kmeanspp} initialization and 10 random restarts per $k$ value.

\paragraph{Cluster Naming.}
MMR exemplar selection uses $K_e = 16$ exemplars with $\lambda = 0.7$. The MLM backend uses BERT-base-uncased. The LLM backend uses Llama-3 served via Ollama.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

We report metrics at three levels of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Entity Detection.}
We report span-level \textbf{Precision}, \textbf{Recall}, and \textbf{F1} on the test set of each target domain, using exact span match. This evaluates whether the multi-view DTrans detector transfers better than the OWNER BIO-only baseline.

\paragraph{Entity Typing.}
We report \textbf{Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI)}~\cite{vinh-etal-2010-ami} to measure clustering quality. AMI is a mapping-free metric: it does not require any cluster-to-type assignment and is robust to permutations of cluster labels, over-segmentation, and over-merging.

Let $U = \{U_1,\ldots,U_R\}$ be the predicted clustering and $V = \{V_1,\ldots,V_C\}$ be the gold partition, both over $N$ entity spans. Define the empirical joint probability $p(i,j) = |U_i \cap V_j|/N$ and the marginals $p_U(i) = |U_i|/N$, $p_V(j) = |V_j|/N$. The \textbf{Mutual Information} between the two clusterings is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MI}(U,V) = \sum_{i=1}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{C} p(i,j)\,\log\frac{p(i,j)}{p_U(i)\,p_V(j)}.
    \label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
MI is large when predicted clusters align well with gold types but is inflated by chance when the number of clusters is large. AMI corrects for this by subtracting the expected MI under a random hypergeometric baseline~\cite{vinh-etal-2010-ami}:
\begin{equation}
    \text{AMI}(U,V) = \frac{\text{MI}(U,V) - \mathbb{E}[\text{MI}(U,V)]}{\frac{1}{2}\bigl[H(U) + H(V)\bigr] - \mathbb{E}[\text{MI}(U,V)]},
    \label{eq:ami}
\end{equation}
where $H(U) = -\sum_i p_U(i)\log p_U(i)$ is the entropy of the predicted partition and $\mathbb{E}[\text{MI}]$ is the analytically derived expectation under the null hypothesis that $U$ and $V$ are drawn independently with fixed cluster sizes. AMI $\approx 0$ indicates random agreement; AMI $= 1$ indicates perfect agreement.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Example.}
Suppose $N = 6$ spans with gold types $V = \{\{s_1,s_2,s_3\},\{s_4,s_5,s_6\}\}$ (two perfect groups of 3) and the predicted clustering $U = \{\{s_1,s_2,s_3\},\{s_4,s_5,s_6\}\}$ perfectly matches. Then $\text{MI}(U,V) = \log 2 \approx 0.693$ and $H(U) = H(V) = \log 2$, giving $\text{AMI} = 1.0$. If instead $U$ randomly splits the spans as $\{\{s_1,s_3,s_5\},\{s_2,s_4,s_6\}\}$, $\text{MI}(U,V)$ approaches $\mathbb{E}[\text{MI}]$ and $\text{AMI} \approx 0$.
\end{quote}

We follow the OWNER paper~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} in using AMI as the primary entity typing metric.

\paragraph{Cluster Naming.}
We evaluate predicted cluster names against gold entity type names using \textbf{BERTScore-F1}~\cite{zhang-etal-2020-bertscore}, which measures semantic similarity between predicted and reference strings using contextual token embeddings from a pretrained language model (RoBERTa-large by default).

Let $\hat{y} = (\hat{y}_1, \ldots, \hat{y}_m)$ be the predicted name tokenised into $m$ tokens and $y = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$ be the reference name tokenised into $n$ tokens. Each token is embedded by RoBERTa-large into a contextual vector $\mathbf{e}$. BERTScore computes pairwise cosine similarities and applies \emph{greedy matching}:
\begin{align}
    P_{\text{BERT}} &= \frac{1}{|\hat{y}|} \sum_{\hat{y}_j \in \hat{y}} \max_{y_i \in y}\, \cos(\mathbf{e}_{\hat{y}_j},\, \mathbf{e}_{y_i}),
    \label{eq:bertscore_p} \\
    R_{\text{BERT}} &= \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_{y_i \in y} \max_{\hat{y}_j \in \hat{y}}\, \cos(\mathbf{e}_{y_i},\, \mathbf{e}_{\hat{y}_j}),
    \label{eq:bertscore_r} \\
    F_{\text{BERT}} &= 2 \cdot \frac{P_{\text{BERT}} \cdot R_{\text{BERT}}}{P_{\text{BERT}} + R_{\text{BERT}}}.
    \label{eq:bertscore_f}
\end{align}
Precision $P_{\text{BERT}}$ measures how well each predicted token matches some reference token; Recall $R_{\text{BERT}}$ measures how well each reference token is covered by some predicted token. Unlike exact-match accuracy, BERTScore awards partial credit for semantically close labels and penalises completely wrong predictions, making it well-suited for evaluating open-vocabulary cluster names.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Example.}
Given candidate \textit{``The cat is sitting on the mat''} and reference \textit{``There is a cat on the mat''}, RoBERTa-large greedily matches each token to its most similar counterpart (\textit{cat}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{cat}, \textit{sitting}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{is}, etc.), yielding $F_{\text{BERT}}=0.953$ despite different word order. By contrast, BLEU gives near-zero for a synonym paraphrase such as \textit{``The fast auburn fox leaps above the sleepy hound''} (reference: \textit{``The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog''}) because no n-gram matches, while BERTScore correctly assigns 0.952. In the NER naming context, \textit{``musician''} vs.\ gold \textit{``musicalartist''} scores $F_{\text{BERT}}\approx0.94$ (semantic credit), whereas a completely wrong prediction scores near the floor of $\approx0.86$.
\end{quote}

Figure~\ref{fig:bertscore_bar} illustrates the score range: exact copies score 1.0, synonym paraphrases score $\approx 0.95$, and completely wrong labels score $\approx 0.86$ with RoBERTa-large embeddings. Because all scores are compressed into the range $[0.86, 1.0]$, differences of even 0.01--0.02 in BERTScore-F1 are meaningful and correspond to discernible semantic proximity.

\section{Baselines}

We compare against the following systems:

\paragraph{OWNER (baseline).}
The original OWNER pipeline~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} uses a DeBERTa-v3-base BIO mention detector trained on the source domain, BERT fill-in-the-blank entity embeddings, BIC-guided $k$-means clustering, and single-template MLM naming without exemplar selection.

\paragraph{Span2Type-CentroidNaming (MLM).}
Uses the centroid-nearest single exemplar for cluster naming instead of MMR-based diverse selection, with the MLM backend. This variant isolates the contribution of exemplar diversity in the naming stage.

\paragraph{Span2Type-MLM (MMR).}
Uses MMR-based exemplar selection with the MLM (BERT) naming backend.

\paragraph{Span2Type-LLM (MMR).}
The proposed cluster naming configuration: MMR-based exemplar selection with the LLM (Llama-3 via Ollama) naming backend.

\paragraph{Span2Type (CoNLL) / Span2Type (Pile-NER).}
The complete pipeline: DTrans multi-view entity detection + two-stage soft-prompt entity typing + BIC $k$-means + MMR-based LLM naming, trained on CoNLL-2003 or Pile-NER respectively.

To isolate the contribution of each component, we evaluate the following ablation variants against the full system:

\end{itemize}

The contribution of each component is isolated in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_study} by progressively removing or replacing one part of the full system.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Per-domain patterns in entity detection.}
Table~\ref{tab:mention_detection} shows that the DTrans multi-view detector (BIO-SE-TB) achieves an average F1 of 82.35 across five CrossNER domains, compared to 75.68 for the OWNER BIO-only baseline---a gain of 6.67 points. However, the improvement is highly uneven across domains and this variation is informative. The largest gain occurs in AI (+20.85 points, from 60.5 to 81.35), a domain whose entity vocabulary includes multi-token technical terms (e.g., \textit{support vector machine}, \textit{convolutional neural network}) with irregular and often ambiguous word boundaries. Single-view BIO tagging must decide the tag of each token in isolation, making it prone to splitting or missing such spans. The Start-End decoder explicitly marks the first and last token of each mention, while the Tie-Break decoder captures whether adjacent tokens co-belong to a span; together they recover entity boundaries that BIO misses. The same pattern holds for Science (+0.97) and Politics (+9.04), which also contain multi-token named entities with diverse surface forms.

Literature is the sole domain where OWNER BIO (83.9) marginally outperforms BIO-SE-TB (82.68, $-1.22$). Literary entity mentions---character names, author surnames, book titles---tend to follow predictable proper-noun patterns that a single sequential BIO tagger handles reliably. In this regime, the SE and TB decoders add noise rather than signal: false-positive span candidates introduced by the union operation slightly reduce precision without a compensating recall gain.

\paragraph{Why competing span detectors underperform.}
PURE (35.76 avg F1) and SpanProto (61.00) were designed for few-shot or prototype-based NER, where per-type exemplars are available at inference time. In the open-world evaluation used here, no target-domain labels are provided at any stage, so these methods operate effectively in a zero-shot regime for which they were not designed. Their poor performance is therefore expected and reflects a fundamental mismatch between method assumptions and evaluation conditions, not an algorithmic weakness. WL-Coref (65.62) performs closer to the BIO baseline because it focuses on span coherence rather than per-type classification, making it more transferable to unseen domains without type annotations.

\paragraph{Cascade effect: detection quality improves end-to-end AMI.}
The main results (Table~\ref{tab:ner_comparison}) show Span2Type (CoNLL) achieves 61.4 average AMI, a 12.0-point improvement over OWNER (49.4).
The ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_study} attributes $+9.2$ of this gain to the DTrans multi-view detector and $+2.8$ to soft-prompt entity typing.
The total end-to-end AMI gain (12.0) also exceeds the detection F1 gain (6.67), which we hypothesise reflects a compounding effect through the pipeline: better span detection reduces the number of false-positive spans passed to the entity typing stage, yielding a cleaner embedding pool for $k$-means clustering, while higher-precision span boundaries likely produce more coherent \texttt{[MASK]}-position representations given the template's sensitivity to correct entity delimitation.
This is a plausible explanation consistent with the observed data; a controlled verification---varying detection precision and recall independently while holding the typing stage fixed---would be needed to confirm the mechanism and is left to future work.

\paragraph{Why LLM prompting baselines underperform.}
Among open-world baselines, UniNER with GPT-4o mini (47.4 AMI) is the strongest, yet still falls 14.0 points below Span2Type (CoNLL). ChatIE lags further at 22.8--35.0. These methods issue a single LLM prompt per sentence asking the model to identify and type entities simultaneously. Their weakness is threefold. First, they lack an explicit span detection stage trained on a labeled source domain, so span recall is limited by the LLM's zero-shot extraction ability. Second, they impose an open-vocabulary label space on the LLM at extraction time, producing inconsistent and noisy type strings that reduce the clustering signal when evaluated against a fixed CrossNER ontology. Third, the AMI metric measures agreement with a gold partition of entity spans; LLM-extracted spans and types differ structurally from ground-truth annotation, creating systematic offset even when the LLM's predictions are semantically correct. Span2Type avoids these issues by separating span detection (trained discriminatively), embedding (template-based), and clustering (unsupervised) into well-defined stages, each optimized for its subtask.

\paragraph{Effect of source corpus on cross-domain transfer.}
Span2Type (Pile-NER) achieves 55.42 AMI on average, 5.98 points below the CoNLL-source variant (61.4). The CrossNER target domains---AI, Literature, Music, Politics, Science---consist primarily of Wikipedia-style sentences about specific topics. CoNLL-2003, despite its narrower entity inventory (4 types), consists of structured newswire text whose sentence syntax, noun-phrase patterns, and capitalization conventions are stylistically closer to Wikipedia than the heterogeneous web-crawled text in Pile-NER. Training on Pile-NER exposes the detector to a broader vocabulary but also to noisier, more colloquial text that degrades the precision of span boundaries on the relatively formal CrossNER sentences. This result cautions against the intuition that a larger, more diverse source corpus always benefits cross-domain transfer: source--target stylistic alignment may matter as much as vocabulary coverage.

\paragraph{Contribution of soft prompt tuning.}
The ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_study}) shows that adding the soft prompt to the DTrans-equipped model improves average AMI from 58.6 to 61.4 (+2.8 points). While modest in absolute terms, this gain comes at near-zero parameter cost: the soft prompt introduces only $5 \times 768 = 3{,}840$ parameters (0.003\% of the BERT encoder), whereas full fine-tuning updates all $\sim$110M. The two-stage training schedule is critical here: Stage~1 warms up the soft prompt tokens so they carry meaningful gradient information before the PLM is unfrozen; Stage~2 then fine-tunes the entire model at a very low learning rate ($1\times10^{-5}$), preserving pre-trained representations while allowing discriminative adaptation. Without Stage~1 warm-up, the PLM would be fine-tuned from random soft token inputs, degrading both the prompt and the backbone. The ``w/o Ensemble MD'' row (57.80), where soft prompt tuning is applied with only a single BIO detector, confirms that the soft prompt contributes independently of the multi-view detector: its 8.4-point gain over OWNER (49.4) is consistent across ablation conditions.

\paragraph{LLM versus MLM naming: structural analysis.}
The BERTScore-F1 results (Table~\ref{tab:naming}) expose a fundamental expressiveness difference between the two naming backends. The MLM backend is constrained to predict a single subword token at the \texttt{[MASK]} position; this means compound CrossNER type labels such as \texttt{musicalartist}, \texttt{literarygenre}, and \texttt{astronomicalobj} can never be predicted exactly, since they are not single vocabulary tokens. The LLM backend generates free-form text and can produce multi-word labels (\textit{political party}, \textit{music genres}, \textit{literary work}) that better match the semantics of compound types. This structural advantage explains most of the $+0.028$ average gap between Span2Type-LLM (MMR) and Span2Type-MLM (MMR).

Span2Type-LLM (MMR) outperforms OWNER (single-template MLM) in all five domains, with the largest gain in Music ($+0.037$) and the smallest in Science ($+0.018$).
In the budget-controlled comparison against OWNER (LLM), Literature is the single domain where OWNER (LLM) (0.923) outperforms Span2Type-LLM (MMR) (0.913).
In Literature, the entity clusters contain distinctive, unambiguous entities---book titles, author names, and awards---whose surface forms provide sufficient signal for the LLM even under random sampling; MMR's diversity constraint offers no additional benefit in this case.
In all other four domains, MMR-based exemplar selection consistently yields higher BERTScore-F1 than OWNER (LLM)'s random selection, confirming the general advantage of diverse exemplar coverage for cluster naming.

\paragraph{Why MMR helps LLM naming more than MLM.}
Span2Type-MLM (MMR) outscores Span2Type-CentroidNaming (MLM) by only $+0.003$ on average, while the LLM variant benefits substantially from diverse exemplar selection (as seen in the larger overall gap). The difference in MMR benefit stems from how each backend aggregates exemplar evidence. The MLM backend independently computes a token probability distribution for each exemplar and averages the distributions (Equation~\ref{eq:mlm_avg}); adding diverse exemplars improves coverage but the single-token constraint caps the gain. The LLM backend reads all exemplars jointly in a single prompt and synthesises a label holistically; diverse exemplars allow the LLM to form a broader semantic picture of the cluster and avoid fixating on the surface form of the centroid-nearest entity. This holistic synthesis amplifies the diversity benefit in domains where cluster membership is semantically diverse; when clusters are tight and homogeneous (as in Music), MMR may select peripheral examples that reduce rather than improve naming quality.

\paragraph{Error analysis and failure modes.}
Three systematic failure patterns are observed across all domains. First, \textbf{specificity mismatch}: the LLM predicts a hyponym of the correct type (\textit{actor} for \texttt{person}; \textit{physicist} by MLM for \texttt{researcher}), because the cluster's exemplar evidence is skewed toward a specific subtype. BERTScore assigns partial credit in these cases, but the semantic distance is non-trivial. Second, \textbf{compound label penalty}: CrossNER uses concatenated compound strings (\texttt{literarygenre}, \texttt{musicalartist}) without whitespace; both the MLM and LLM produce natural multi-word labels that are semantically correct yet receive reduced BERTScore due to the tokenisation mismatch between predicted label and reference string. Third, \textbf{MLM hallucination}: a small number of clusters across domains receive the token \textit{vegetarian} from the MLM backend. This artefact occurs when the entity context is out-of-distribution for BERT's pretraining data, causing the model to default to a high-frequency but semantically unrelated token. The LLM backend is immune to this artefact because its generative process is conditioned on the entity list directly rather than on a fill-in-the-blank template.

The near-zero correlation ($r{=}0.01$) between cluster purity and BERTScore-F1 (Figure~\ref{fig:naming_summary}) indicates that naming quality is largely decoupled from clustering quality. Even when a cluster contains entities of mixed true types, the LLM can often identify the dominant type from the exemplar evidence and assign a semantically accurate label. This resilience suggests that the naming module can compensate for imperfect clustering, and that improving clustering quality (e.g., via better $k$ estimation) would primarily benefit the AMI metric rather than naming quality.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{sec:Con}

This thesis presented \textbf{Span2Type}, an improved open-world NER pipeline built on the OWNER framework, with three targeted enhancements addressing the two weakest points of the original system: span detection quality and automatic entity type naming.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

We introduced three improvements to the OWNER pipeline and evaluated them systematically on the CrossNER benchmark across five target domains.

\textbf{First}, we replaced OWNER's single BIO mention detector with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which jointly trains BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break decoders on the source domain with a Mean Teaching strategy. This change alone improved span detection F1 from an average of 75.68 (OWNER BIO) to 82.35 (BIO-SE-TB), a gain of 6.67 points across five CrossNER domains.

\textbf{Second}, we introduced a two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning approach for the entity typing stage. Stage~1 optimizes only $N_p = 5$ soft token embeddings ($\sim$3,840 parameters) while the BERT backbone is frozen, using a high learning rate ($3\times10^{-3}$) for rapid convergence. At epoch $E_{\text{unfreeze}} = 10$, Stage~2 unfreezes the backbone and continues training at a much lower learning rate ($1\times10^{-5}$) to prevent catastrophic forgetting, with the optimizer's accumulated moment estimates for the soft prompt preserved via \texttt{add\_param\_group()}. This two-stage strategy achieves parameter efficiency in early training and full-model expressiveness in later epochs, contributing a +2.8-point gain in entity typing AMI.

\textbf{Third}, we proposed an MMR-based exemplar selection strategy for automatic cluster naming. Rather than querying a naming model with the single centroid-nearest example per cluster, we use Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} to select $K_e = 16$ diverse, centroid-relevant exemplars before passing them to either an MLM or a locally hosted LLM. This ensures the naming model receives evidence spanning the full semantic range of the cluster, producing more specific and stable type names.

On the CrossNER benchmark, Span2Type (CoNLL source) achieves an average end-to-end NER AMI of \textbf{61.4}, compared to \textbf{49.4} for the OWNER baseline—a 12.0-point improvement. Ablation experiments confirm that the multi-view detector contributes the largest share of this gain (+9.2 points), while soft prompt tuning provides a complementary additional improvement (+2.8 points).

\section{Limitations}

Despite these improvements, Span2Type retains several limitations inherited from the OWNER framework.

The entity detector is still trained on a labeled source domain and transferred to target domains; in settings where the source and target domains are very distant (e.g., newswire to clinical text), transfer quality degrades. Future work could explore self-training or domain-adaptive pretraining to bridge larger domain gaps.

The BIC-guided $k$ selection works well when the true number of entity types falls within the search range $[k_{\min}, k_{\max}]$, but it can still misestimate $k$ for domains with highly imbalanced type distributions or very fine-grained entity inventories. A more principled nonparametric clustering approach could improve robustness here.

The cluster naming quality depends on the availability and quality of the MLM or LLM backend. Single-token MLM names are sometimes too generic (e.g., \textit{entity}, \textit{person}), while LLM-based names, though richer, require a locally hosted model that may not always be accessible. Improving the post-processing and filtering of generated names remains an open problem.

\section{Future Work}

Several directions extend the work presented in this thesis. First, the soft prompt tuning approach could be extended with adapter layers or LoRA-based fine-tuning~\cite{hu-etal-2022-lora} to capture more expressive adaptation with a still-limited parameter budget. Second, the MMR exemplar selection strategy could be applied not only at naming time but also during the entity typing training phase, selecting diverse in-context examples for few-shot or retrieval-augmented typing. Third, the BIC-guided clustering could be replaced with a hierarchical or density-based method that produces a type hierarchy rather than a flat partition, enabling more nuanced ontology induction. Finally, Span2Type could be evaluated on additional benchmarks beyond CrossNER, particularly in biomedical and legal domains where the need for open-world entity type discovery is most acute.
