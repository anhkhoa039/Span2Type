\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch-Introduction}

Named Entity Recognition (NER) is a foundational task in natural language processing (NLP), underpinning a wide range of downstream applications including information extraction, knowledge graph construction, question answering, biomedical text mining, and document understanding. The objective of NER is to locate mentions of named entities in text and classify them into predefined semantic categories such as \textsc{Person}, \textsc{Organization}, or \textsc{Location}. Over the past decade, advances in deep learning and pretrained language models have pushed NER performance to near-human levels on standard benchmarks such as CoNLL-2003~\cite{tjong-kim-sang-de-meulder-2003-introduction} and OntoNotes~\cite{pradhan-etal-2013-towards}.

Despite this progress, virtually all high-performing NER systems operate under a \emph{closed-world assumption}: the set of entity types is fixed and known in advance, and models are trained on large amounts of manually annotated data conforming to that type inventory. This assumption, while convenient for benchmark evaluation, creates a fundamental barrier to deploying NER in practice. In real-world settings, new domains constantly emerge---scientific subfields, legal jurisdictions, industrial verticals, social media communities---each with its own vocabulary of relevant entities that may bear little resemblance to the coarse types defined in general-domain corpora. Annotating sufficient labeled data for every new domain is expensive, slow, and often requires specialized expert knowledge. Furthermore, even within a single domain, entity categories are not static: terminology evolves, new entity types emerge, and the granularity of the desired ontology varies by application.

These limitations motivate a shift toward \emph{open-world NER}: a paradigm in which the system receives an unannotated corpus and is expected to (1)~identify entity mention spans, (2)~group those spans into coherent semantic categories without being told how many categories exist or what they are called, and (3)~produce human-readable names for the discovered categories. The third requirement---\emph{automatic entity type naming}---is often treated as an afterthought in existing work, yet it is critical for practical deployment. Clusters of entity mentions without interpretable labels cannot be validated by domain experts, integrated into knowledge bases, or used as supervision signals for downstream tasks. Naming is therefore not merely a cosmetic concern; it is what transforms unsupervised cluster assignments into a usable, inspectable entity type ontology.

This thesis presents \textbf{Span2Type}, an improved open-world NER pipeline built on top of the OWNER framework~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner}, with two targeted enhancements. Span2Type operates in three stages. First, the mention detection module from OWNER---which uses a single BIO sequence labeling head---is replaced with the Entity Detection module from the Divide-and-Transfer (DTrans) paradigm~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}. The DTrans module uses BERT with three complementary tagging schemes applied simultaneously: standard BIO tagging for global span context, Start-End (SE) binary classifiers for explicit boundary detection, and Tie-Break (TB) classifiers that model whether adjacent tokens belong to the same span. Training all three views jointly with a Mean Teaching strategy (exponential moving average) improves domain transfer robustness, since entity span boundaries are more stable across domains than entity types. Second, the entity typing module encodes each detected span using the fill-in-the-blank template \emph{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}.''}\ and extracts the \texttt{[MASK]}-position hidden state from a BERT-based encoder. This thesis introduces a soft prompt variant of this stage: a small number of trainable soft token embeddings are prepended to the template input while the base encoder is kept frozen, so that only the prompt parameters (not the full model) are updated during triplet margin training. The resulting embeddings are clustered with automatic $k$ selection via the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Third, the cluster naming module---the main novel contribution of this thesis---selects a diverse, representative set of exemplar sentences for each cluster using Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) and applies either a masked language model or a locally hosted large language model (LLM) to generate a concise, human-readable type name by aggregating predictions over the selected exemplars.

Span2Type requires no predefined type inventory and no target-domain annotations. The mention detector is trained on a source-domain corpus (CoNLL-2003 or Pile-NER~\cite{zhou-etal-2024-universalner}) and transferred to new domains without retraining. Experiments on CrossNER spanning five target domains demonstrate that replacing OWNER's BIO-only mention detector with the DTrans multi-view entity detector improves span recall across domains, and that MMR-based exemplar selection produces more specific and stable cluster names compared to OWNER's original naming procedure.

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We integrate the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans} into the OWNER open-world NER pipeline, replacing its single BIO mention detector with a joint BIO + Start-End + Tie-Break decoder that yields higher span recall in cross-domain settings.
    \item We propose an automatic cluster naming procedure that uses Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} to select a diverse set of exemplar sentences per cluster before querying an MLM or LLM, improving naming quality and stability over the centroid-nearest baseline used in the original OWNER system.
    \item We provide a systematic empirical evaluation on CrossNER across five target domains, comparing the improved pipeline against the original OWNER baseline and reporting entity detection F1, entity typing AMI, and cluster naming quality via BERTScore.
\end{itemize}


\section{Motivation} \label{sec:Challenges}

The development of Span2Type is driven by three concrete challenges that arise when attempting to deploy NER systems beyond well-curated benchmark settings.

\paragraph{Challenge 1: Robust span detection across domains without target supervision.}
The first stage of any open-world NER pipeline is detecting entity mention spans in the target corpus without access to target-domain labels. The standard approach, used in OWNER, is to train a BIO sequence tagger on a labeled source domain (e.g., CoNLL-2003) and apply it directly to the target domain. While effective in closely related domains, single-view BIO taggers struggle when target domain text differs substantially in structure or vocabulary from the source. Entity boundary signals---such as whether a token starts a new mention or is connected to the preceding token---can be captured from multiple complementary perspectives. A multi-view detector that jointly trains BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break decoders can exploit these different signals simultaneously, producing span candidates that are more robust to domain shift. This thesis addresses this challenge by replacing OWNER's BIO-only mention detector with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}.

\paragraph{Challenge 2: Unknown and evolving entity type inventories.}
Even in domains where NER data exist, the type inventory may be incomplete or misaligned with the application's needs. Standard NER benchmarks use small, coarse-grained label sets (typically four to eighteen types). Many real-world applications require finer granularity---distinguishing, for example, between \textsc{Drug}, \textsc{Chemical}, and \textsc{Disease} rather than a single \textsc{Medical} type---or operate on entity categories that have no counterpart in existing ontologies, such as entities specific to proprietary product catalogs or newly coined technical terms. Purely supervised methods cannot discover new types; they can only classify entities into the types they were trained on. Open-world NER, by contrast, makes no assumptions about the number or nature of entity types and can surface latent categories that would be missed by any closed-world system.

\paragraph{Challenge 3: The interpretability gap in unsupervised entity clustering.}
Existing unsupervised entity typing methods typically produce cluster assignments without associated type names. The output of such a system is a mapping from entity spans to cluster identifiers (e.g., \textit{Cluster 3}, \textit{Cluster 7}), which carries no semantic content. Practitioners who wish to use these assignments must manually inspect cluster members, guess an appropriate label, and verify that the label is consistent across the cluster---a process that reintroduces the very human effort that unsupervised methods were supposed to eliminate. Automatic type naming bridges this interpretability gap: by generating a concise label such as \textsc{Pharmaceutical Company} or \textsc{Protein Complex} directly from cluster evidence, the system enables human experts to validate the discovered ontology and integrate it into downstream pipelines with minimal manual intervention.

Together, these three challenges motivate the two improvements this thesis makes to the OWNER pipeline. The DTrans multi-view entity detector addresses Challenge~1 by capturing complementary span boundary signals that transfer better across domains. The MMR-based cluster naming module addresses Challenge~3 by producing human-readable type names from diverse cluster evidence rather than leaving practitioners with anonymous cluster identifiers. Challenge~2---the unknown number of entity types---is handled by the BIC-guided $k$ selection already present in OWNER, which this thesis retains unchanged.


\section{Contributions} \label{sec:Overview}

This thesis makes the following specific technical contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-view entity detection for open-world NER.} We replace the BIO-only mention detector in OWNER with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which jointly trains three complementary decoders---BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break---on the source domain. The Mean Teaching strategy (exponential moving average) further stabilizes training, yielding more reliable span candidates when transferred to unseen target domains.

    \item \textbf{Two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning for entity typing.} We introduce a parameter-efficient variant of the entity typing stage that trains in two stages. Stage~1 prepends $N_p$ trainable soft token embeddings to the fill-in-the-blank template and optimizes only these tokens at a high learning rate while the BERT backbone is strictly frozen, allowing rapid convergence without perturbing pre-trained weights. Stage~2 unfreezes the backbone at a drastically lower learning rate ($1\times10^{-5}$) to prevent catastrophic forgetting, while the optimizer's accumulated first- and second-moment statistics for the soft prompt are preserved via \texttt{add\_param\_group()}. This two-stage schedule achieves the parameter efficiency of prompt-only tuning in early epochs and the full expressiveness of fine-tuning in later epochs.

    \item \textbf{MMR-based exemplar selection for automatic cluster naming.} We propose selecting a diverse, centroid-relevant set of exemplar sentences per cluster using Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} before querying either an MLM or a locally hosted LLM for type name generation. Compared to OWNER's original naming procedure, MMR-based selection reduces exemplar redundancy and produces more specific and stable cluster names.

    \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation on CrossNER.} We evaluate the improved pipeline on five CrossNER target domains~\cite{liu-etal-2021-crossner}, reporting span detection F1, entity typing Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) and Macro-F1, and cluster naming quality via BERTScore, with ablations that isolate the contribution of each component.
\end{itemize}


\nochapterspacing{Related Work}\label{ch:Work}

\section{Supervised Named Entity Recognition}
Named Entity Recognition has a long history as a sequence labeling problem. Early systems relied on hand-crafted features and probabilistic models such as Hidden Markov Models and Conditional Random Fields~(CRFs)~\cite{lafferty-etal-2001-crf}. The introduction of bidirectional LSTM-CRF architectures~\cite{ma-hovy-2016-end} significantly improved performance by learning contextual token representations without manual feature engineering. The transformer architecture~\cite{vaswani-etal-2017-attention} and pretrained models such as BERT~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} and RoBERTa~\cite{liu-etal-2019-roberta} have become the de facto backbone for NER, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks including CoNLL-2003~\cite{tjong-kim-sang-de-meulder-2003-introduction} and OntoNotes~\cite{pradhan-etal-2013-towards}. Span-based models~\cite{zhong-chen-2021-frustratingly} offer an alternative to token-level BIO labeling by directly enumerating and classifying candidate spans. All of these approaches share a common limitation: they require annotated data for a fixed, predefined set of entity types and cannot generalize to type inventories unseen during training.

\section{Cross-domain and Low-resource NER}
A line of work addresses the cost of annotation by transferring NER models across domains or reducing the amount of supervision required. Cross-domain NER studies explore domain adaptation techniques, multi-task learning, and data augmentation to transfer models trained on newswire to specialized domains such as biomedical or social media text~\cite{derczynski-etal-2017-results}. Few-shot NER approaches~\cite{yang-katiyar-2020-simple, ma-etal-2022-label} use prototypical networks or meta-learning to recognize new entity types from a handful of examples. While these methods reduce the annotation burden, they still assume that the target type inventory is specified in advance and that at least a small number of labeled examples per type is available. In contrast, this thesis targets the strictly unsupervised setting where no target-domain labels of any kind are provided.

\section{Open-world and Unsupervised Entity Typing}
Open-world NER removes the closed-world assumption entirely: the system must discover entity types from raw text without any predefined ontology or labeled examples. Representative work in this space embeds entity mentions using pretrained language models and applies clustering to group them by type. Fill-in-the-blank (cloze) templates have been proposed as a way to obtain type-sensitive embeddings by exploiting the knowledge stored in masked language models~\cite{petroni-etal-2019-language, jiang-etal-2020-know}. More recent systems such as UniversalNER~\cite{zhou-etal-2024-universalner} and GLiNER~\cite{li-etal-2023-gliner} use instruction-tuned LLMs to perform open-vocabulary NER, while ChatIE~\cite{wei-etal-2023-chatie} applies zero-shot prompting. The OWNER framework~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} is a representative clustering-based pipeline: it trains a DeBERTa-based~\cite{he-etal-2021-deberta} mention detector on a source domain and clusters BERT \texttt{[MASK]}-position embeddings on the target domain using $k$-means with automatic $k$ selection via the Bayesian Information Criterion~(BIC). This thesis builds directly on OWNER and focuses on improving its span detection and cluster naming components.

\section{Fine-grained and Ultra-fine Entity Typing}
Fine-grained and ultra-fine entity typing~\cite{choi-etal-2018-ultra, onoe-durrett-2020-interpretable} assign more detailed semantic types to entity mentions, often using large type vocabularies derived from knowledge bases. While these approaches provide richer type information than coarse NER, they still rely on a predefined type ontology and treat entity typing as a supervised classification problem. They do not address the open-world challenge of discovering new entity types without any labeled data.

\section{Clustering Methods for Entity Typing}
Most embedding-based entity typing systems rely on $k$-means~\cite{macqueen-1967-kmeans} or its variants for cluster assignment. The $k$-means++ initialization~\cite{arthur-vassilvitskii-2007-kmeanspp} significantly improves convergence stability over random initialization. A practical challenge is that the correct number of clusters $k$ is unknown in the open-world setting. The Bayesian Information Criterion~\cite{schwarz-1978-bic} penalizes model complexity and provides a principled trade-off between fit quality and parsimony. Clustering quality is measured using the Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI)~\cite{vinh-etal-2010-ami}, a metric robust to over-segmentation and over-merging. This thesis uses BIC-guided $k$-means as implemented in OWNER, treating cluster selection as a fixed component and concentrating improvements on detection and naming.

\section{Masked Language Model Probing for Type Inference}
Masked language models such as BERT have been widely used as knowledge bases that can be queried via cloze prompts to infer semantic properties of entities~\cite{petroni-etal-2019-language}. For entity typing, the standard approach is to construct a template such as \emph{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}’’} and take the top-ranked token at the mask position as the predicted type~\cite{jiang-etal-2020-know}. The quality of this prediction is sensitive to prompt design: different templates yield different tokens, and the top-ranked token is often a generic or uninformative word. Aggregating predictions over multiple instances of the same cluster, rather than relying on a single probe, improves stability. This thesis adopts MLM probing as the primary naming backend and proposes a diversity-aware exemplar selection strategy to improve the quality of aggregate predictions.

\section{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Soft Prompts}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining competitive performance. Prefix-tuning~\cite{li-liang-2021-prefix} and P-tuning~\cite{liu-etal-2022-ptuning} prepend trainable continuous tokens to the input while keeping the backbone frozen. The Power of Scale work~\cite{lester-etal-2021-power} shows that prompt tuning becomes competitive with full fine-tuning at larger model scales. LoRA~\cite{hu-etal-2022-lora} injects low-rank update matrices into attention layers. These methods are particularly relevant in open-world NER where training data from the source domain may be limited, motivating the soft prompt variant introduced in this thesis.

\section{Large Language Models for Cluster Labeling}
The emergence of large language models~\cite{brown-etal-2020-language} has opened a new avenue for cluster labeling: instead of querying a masked language model, one can prompt a generative model with a set of representative cluster examples and ask it to produce a category name. Systems such as UniversalNER~\cite{zhou-etal-2024-universalner} and GOLLiE~\cite{sainz-etal-2023-gollie} demonstrate the versatility of LLMs for open-vocabulary information extraction. The main practical challenge is selecting which examples to include in the prompt: redundant examples waste context and introduce noise, while centroid-only selection may miss the full semantic range of the cluster. This thesis addresses this with Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} for exemplar selection.

\section{Maximal Marginal Relevance}
Maximal Marginal Relevance was originally proposed for query-based document summarization and result set diversification in information retrieval~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use}. MMR iteratively selects items that are both relevant to a query and dissimilar to already-selected items, controlled by a trade-off parameter $\lambda$. It has since been applied to sentence selection in extractive summarization, example selection for in-context learning, and retrieval-augmented generation. In this thesis, MMR selects a diverse subset of entity mention sentences from each cluster, with the cluster centroid serving as the query, ensuring the naming model receives evidence spanning the cluster’s full semantic range.

\section{Positioning of This Work}
This thesis does not propose a new open-world NER framework from scratch. Instead, it takes the OWNER pipeline as the base system and improves two components: the span detection stage is upgraded from a BIO-only head to the multi-view DTrans detector~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, and the cluster naming stage is enhanced with MMR-based diverse exemplar selection and a soft prompt entity typing variant. Compared to the original OWNER naming procedure, which uses a single nearest-centroid template per cluster, the proposed approach produces more specific, stable, and interpretable type names across all five CrossNER~\cite{liu-etal-2021-crossner} target domains.



\nochapterspacing{Methodology}\label{sec:Method}

\section{System Overview}

Span2Type is a three-stage pipeline for open-world NER that takes an unannotated target-domain corpus as input and produces a set of entity spans, each assigned to a human-readable entity type. The three stages are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Entity Detection}: Given a sentence, detect all entity mention spans using a multi-view span detector trained on a labeled source domain and transferred to the target domain without retraining.
    \item \textbf{Entity Typing}: Encode each detected span using a fill-in-the-blank template and a pretrained masked language model, then cluster the resulting embeddings to assign unsupervised type identifiers. A soft prompt variant replaces full fine-tuning with parameter-efficient prompt tuning.
    \item \textbf{Automatic Cluster Naming}: Assign a concise, human-readable name to each cluster by selecting diverse representative exemplars via Maximal Marginal Relevance and querying either an MLM or a large language model for consensus type predictions.
\end{enumerate}

The pipeline assumes no target-domain labels at any stage. Stages 2 and 3 are applied entirely to the target domain. Stage 1 uses source-domain labels only for training the entity detector. Figure~\ref{fig:span2type_framework} illustrates the overall architecture. Table~\ref{table:notations} summarizes the notation used throughout this chapter.

\section{Stage 1: Multi-View Entity Detection}

\subsection{Motivation}

The original OWNER pipeline uses a DeBERTa-v3-base encoder with a single BIO sequence labeling head to detect entity spans. While BIO tagging is effective when the source and target domains are similar, it captures only one view of span boundary information. An entity mention can be characterized from at least three complementary perspectives: its internal BIO label sequence, the positions of its start and end tokens, and the pairwise ``tied or broken'' relationship between adjacent tokens. Using all three simultaneously produces richer and more transferable span representations.

This thesis replaces OWNER's BIO-only detector with the Entity Detection (ED) module from the Divide-and-Transfer (DTrans) paradigm~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which implements exactly this multi-view strategy.

\subsection{Architecture}

The ED module uses BERT-base-cased~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} as its backbone encoder. Given an input sequence $\mathbf{x}$, the encoder produces contextual representations $\mathbf{H} = (\mathbf{h}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_T) \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d}$. Three independent classification heads are applied to $\mathbf{H}$ in parallel:

\paragraph{BIO decoder.}
A linear classifier maps each token hidden state to a three-class distribution over $\{B, I, O\}$:
\begin{equation}
    p^{\text{BIO}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{bio}}\,\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_{\text{bio}}),
    \label{eq:bio}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_{\text{bio}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times d}$. This head captures the global sequential context of entity spans.

\paragraph{Start-End (SE) decoder.}
Two binary classifiers independently predict whether each token is the start or end of an entity span. The end classifier conditions on the predicted start probability to improve boundary coherence:
\begin{equation}
    p^{\text{start}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{start}}\,\mathbf{h}_t), \qquad
    p^{\text{end}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{end}}\,[\mathbf{h}_t; \hat{p}^{\text{start}}_t]),
    \label{eq:se}
\end{equation}
where $[\cdot\,;\,\cdot]$ denotes concatenation and $\hat{p}^{\text{start}}_t$ is the soft start label (one-hot during training, softmax output during inference). This head provides explicit boundary position signals that complement the sequential BIO labels.

\paragraph{Tie-Break (TB) decoder.}
A binary classifier predicts whether each adjacent pair of tokens $(t, t{+}1)$ belongs to the same entity span (\textit{Tie}) or crosses a boundary (\textit{Break}). The hidden representation for the pair is computed as the element-wise sum of the hidden states of both tokens:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h}^{\text{pair}}_t = \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{h}_{t+1}, \qquad
    p^{\text{tb}}_t = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{tb}}\,\mathbf{h}^{\text{pair}}_t).
    \label{eq:tb}
\end{equation}
This head explicitly models token-level adjacency within spans, capturing information that BIO and SE alone may miss.

\subsection{Training Objective}

The model is trained on the labeled source domain. The total loss is the sum of the cross-entropy losses from all three decoders:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{ED}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BIO}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{TB}},
    \label{eq:ed_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SE}} = (\mathcal{L}_{\text{start}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{end}}) / 2$.

To stabilize training and improve generalization, a \textbf{Mean Teaching} strategy based on Exponential Moving Average (EMA) is applied. A teacher model $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ maintains a running average of the student model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_t = \alpha\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t-1} + (1 - \alpha)\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_t,
    \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha = 0.995$. The teacher generates soft pseudo-labels on unlabeled data, which are added as an auxiliary consistency loss weighted by $\lambda_w$, encouraging the student to produce stable predictions.

\subsection{Inference}

At inference time, the three decoders independently decode span candidates from their respective predictions. The final entity span set is the union of candidates from all three decoders after applying a confidence threshold $\delta$ to filter low-confidence predictions. Spans from different views that overlap are merged, giving the downstream entity typing stage a high-recall set of candidates.


\section{Stage 2: Entity Typing}

\subsection{Fill-in-the-Blank Entity Representation}

For each detected entity span $s = (i, j)$ in sentence $\mathbf{x}$, the entity typing stage constructs a type-sensitive representation by filling a template. The template used throughout this thesis is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{template}(s, \mathbf{x}) = \texttt{[CLS] } x_1 \cdots x_T \texttt{ } m \texttt{ is a [MASK] . [SEP]},
    \label{eq:template}
\end{equation}
where $m = x_i \cdots x_j$ is the surface form of the entity mention. This template is tokenized and passed through a BERT-based encoder. The hidden state at the \texttt{[MASK]} position serves as the entity embedding $\mathbf{e}_s \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}_s = \text{Dropout}(\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[MASK]}}),
    \label{eq:mask_emb}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[MASK]}}$ is the last-layer hidden state at the mask position. Because the template implicitly asks the model to predict the type category of the entity, the \texttt{[MASK]}-position representation is more type-discriminative than a raw span embedding.

\subsection{Training with Batch Triplet Margin Loss}

The entity encoder is fine-tuned on the source-domain training set using a \textbf{Batch Triplet Margin Loss}~\cite{schroff-etal-2015-facenet}. For a batch of $N$ entities with embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}_{i=1}^N$ and source-domain type labels $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^N$, all valid $(anchor, positive, negative)$ triplets are extracted in-batch. A triplet $(i, j, k)$ is valid if $y_i = y_j$ and $y_i \neq y_k$. The loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{triplet}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|} \sum_{(i,j,k) \in \mathcal{T}} \max\!\left(0,\; \|\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_j\|_2 - \|\mathbf{e}_i - \mathbf{e}_k\|_2 + \gamma\right),
    \label{eq:triplet}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ is the set of valid triplets and $\gamma = 1.0$ is the margin. This objective pushes embeddings of the same type closer together and embeddings of different types further apart, yielding a metric space where $k$-means clustering recovers semantically coherent entity type groups.

\subsection{Soft Prompt Variant}

Standard fine-tuning updates all parameters of the BERT encoder, which can be expensive and may overfit when training data is limited. This thesis also proposes a \textbf{soft prompt} variant inspired by P-tuning~\cite{liu-etal-2022-ptuning, lester-etal-2021-power}, where the base encoder is kept frozen and a small set of $N_p$ trainable soft token embeddings $\mathbf{P} = [\mathbf{p}_1; \ldots; \mathbf{p}_{N_p}] \in \mathbb{R}^{N_p \times d}$ is prepended to the token embedding sequence before being passed to the frozen encoder:
\begin{equation}
    \text{inputs\_embeds} = [\mathbf{P};\; \mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{x}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{(N_p + L) \times d},
    \label{eq:soft_prompt}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{x}}$ are the standard token embeddings of the template-expanded input of length $L$. The attention mask is extended accordingly to cover the prepended soft tokens. The \texttt{[MASK]}-position index is shifted by $N_p$ to account for the prepended tokens. With $N_p = 5$ (default), this reduces the number of trainable parameters from $\sim$110M to just $5 \times 768 = 3{,}840$.

The same batch triplet margin loss (Equation~\ref{eq:triplet}) is used to train the soft prompt parameters.

\paragraph{Two-Stage Prompt-Initialized Fine-Tuning.}
A naive approach would either keep the backbone frozen for the entire run (limiting expressiveness) or fine-tune it from the first epoch at a standard learning rate (risking catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge~\cite{kirkpatrick-etal-2017-overcoming}). This thesis proposes a \textbf{two-stage} schedule controlled by a threshold epoch $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$:

\textbf{Stage~1 — Soft Prompt Warmup} (epochs $1$ to $E_{\text{unfreeze}} - 1$): The PLM is strictly frozen and the AdamW optimizer tracks only $\mathbf{P}$. A high learning rate $\eta_{\text{prompt}}^{(1)} = 3 \times 10^{-3}$ allows the prompt to converge rapidly in the low-dimensional parameter space without perturbing the pre-trained backbone.

\textbf{Stage~2 — Full Fine-Tuning} (epochs $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$ to $E_{\text{total}}$): The PLM is unfrozen and its parameters are added to the optimizer as a new parameter group at a drastically lower learning rate:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\text{PLM}}^{(2)} = 1 \times 10^{-5} \;\ll\; \eta_{\text{prompt}}^{(1)},
    \label{eq:two_stage_lr}
\end{equation}
The large disparity between learning rates is essential: a high PLM learning rate at Stage~2 would overwrite the pre-trained representations, degrading generalization on unseen domains.

Two implementation details are critical for correctness. First, the PLM parameters are added to the existing optimizer via \texttt{add\_param\_group()} rather than reinitializing the full optimizer. This preserves the first- and second-moment estimates ($m_t$, $v_t$) accumulated for $\mathbf{P}$ during Stage~1 — meaningful gradient statistics for the already-trained soft tokens — while the newly added PLM parameters receive fresh zero-initialized moments, which is appropriate since they have not yet been updated. Second, the learning rate scheduler is reset at the $E_{\text{unfreeze}}$ boundary so that $\eta_{\text{PLM}}^{(2)}$ decays from its full value rather than inheriting a mid-schedule multiplier left over from Stage~1.

\section{Stage 3: BIC-Guided Automatic Cluster Assignment}

\subsection{AutoKmeans}

After the encoder is trained, all entity spans in the target domain are encoded into embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_s\}$. Since the number of entity types in the target domain is unknown, the system must determine $k$ automatically. This is achieved via \textbf{AutoKmeans}, which searches over a range $[k_{\min}, k_{\max}]$ with step $k_{\text{step}}$ and selects the $k^*$ that minimizes the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

For a fitted $k$-means model~\cite{macqueen-1967-kmeans} with inertia $\mathcal{I}(k)$ (sum of squared distances to cluster centroids) on $n$ samples, the BIC~\cite{schwarz-1978-bic} is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{BIC}(k) = n \cdot \log\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{I}(k)}{n}\right) + \log(n) \cdot k.
    \label{eq:bic}
\end{equation}
The first term measures fit quality: it decreases as $k$ grows and the clustering better approximates the data. The second term is a complexity penalty that increases with $k$. The optimal number of clusters is:
\begin{equation}
    k^* = \arg\min_{k \in \{k_{\min},\, k_{\min}+k_{\text{step}},\, \ldots,\, k_{\max}\}} \text{BIC}(k).
    \label{eq:k_star}
\end{equation}
GridSearchCV with $k$-means++ initialization and 10 random restarts is used for each candidate $k$. The best model (at $k^*$) is then used to assign cluster labels $c_s \in \{1, \ldots, k^*\}$ to every entity span.


\section{Stage 4: Automatic Cluster Naming}

\subsection{MMR-Based Exemplar Selection}

Given a cluster $c$ with entity spans $\mathcal{E}_c$ and their embeddings, the naming module first selects a small set of $K_e$ diverse, centroid-relevant exemplar sentences using \textbf{Maximal Marginal Relevance}~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use}.

Let $\boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{E}_c|}\sum_{s \in \mathcal{E}_c} \mathbf{e}_s$ be the cluster centroid. MMR iteratively builds the exemplar set $\mathcal{R}$ by selecting at each step the span that maximizes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{MMR}(s) = \lambda \cdot \cos(\mathbf{e}_s, \boldsymbol{\mu}_c) - (1-\lambda) \cdot \max_{r \in \mathcal{R}} \cos(\mathbf{e}_s, \mathbf{e}_r),
    \label{eq:mmr}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ controls the trade-off between centroid relevance (first term) and diversity relative to already-selected exemplars (second term). A value of $\lambda = 0.7$ is used by default, slightly favouring relevance over diversity. The process repeats until $|\mathcal{R}| = K_e$ (default $K_e = 5$). This ensures that the selected exemplars cover the semantic spread of the cluster rather than all clustering near the centroid.

\subsection{MLM-Based Naming}

In the MLM backend, each selected exemplar sentence $x^{(r)}$ containing entity mention $m^{(r)}$ is inserted into a naming template, e.g.:
\begin{equation*}
    \texttt{In this sentence, } m^{(r)} \texttt{ is a [MASK]. Sentence: } x^{(r)}.
\end{equation*}
The template is tokenized and passed through a pretrained BERT MLM. The probability distribution over the vocabulary at the \texttt{[MASK]} position is extracted, and the distributions from all $K_e$ exemplars are averaged:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{p}_v = \frac{1}{K_e} \sum_{r=1}^{K_e} p_v^{(r)}, \quad v \in \mathcal{V}.
    \label{eq:mlm_avg}
\end{equation}
The candidate tokens are filtered to remove sub-word pieces, special tokens, punctuation, and common stopwords. The top-ranked remaining token is selected as the cluster name. This consensus over diverse exemplars produces more stable and specific names than querying a single representative instance.

\subsection{LLM-Based Naming}

In the LLM backend, the $K_e$ MMR-selected entity mentions are collected into a set $\{e_1, \ldots, e_{K_e}\}$ and sent to a locally hosted language model (via Ollama) using the two-turn prompt from the OWNER paper (Fig.~3 in~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner}):

\begin{quote}
\small
\textbf{System:} \textit{A virtual assistant answers questions from a user based on the provided text.}\\[4pt]
\textbf{User:} \textit{Given the list of entities $\{e_1, \ldots, e_{K_e}\}$, which all belong to the same type, predict the entity type corresponding to all these entities. Respond only with the name of the entity type.}
\end{quote}

The model is queried with temperature~0 for deterministic output. The raw response is post-processed to retain at most three words, strip filler phrases, and normalize to lowercase. Unlike the MLM backend, the LLM can produce multi-word type names (e.g., \textsc{research institute}) that a single \texttt{[MASK]} token cannot express.

The two naming backends are interchangeable within the pipeline; ablation experiments in Chapter~4 compare their naming quality.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

\paragraph{Entity Detection.}
The DTrans multi-view entity detector uses \textbf{BERT-base-cased}~\cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert} as the backbone encoder (12 layers, hidden size 768). Training hyperparameters follow~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}: learning rate $1\times10^{-5}$, batch size 32, 50 epochs, warmup steps 500, EMA decay $\alpha = 0.995$, consistency weight 100. The maximum sequence length is 128 tokens. Experiments are run with two GPUs.

\paragraph{Entity Typing.}
The entity encoding model uses \textbf{BERT-base-uncased} with the template \emph{``\{sentence\} \{entity\} is a \texttt{[MASK]}.''}\ and a batch triplet margin loss ($\gamma = 1.0$). For the standard fine-tuning variant, AdamW with learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ is used for 30 epochs. For the soft prompt variant, two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning (Section~3.3) is applied over 30 epochs: Stage~1 trains only the $N_p = 5$ soft prompt tokens at $\eta = 3\times10^{-3}$ for 10 epochs with the PLM strictly frozen; Stage~2 unfreezes the PLM and fine-tunes both prompt and backbone at $1\times10^{-5}$ for the remaining 20 epochs, with the learning rate scheduler reset at the boundary. Both stages use a linear decay schedule with no warmup steps.

\paragraph{Clustering.}
AutoKmeans searches $k \in [2, 30]$ with step 2, using $k$-means++ initialization and 10 random restarts per $k$ value.

\paragraph{Cluster Naming.}
MMR exemplar selection uses $K_e = 5$ exemplars with $\lambda = 0.7$. The MLM backend uses BERT-base-uncased. The LLM backend uses Llama-3 served via Ollama.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}

We report metrics at two levels of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Entity Detection.}
We report span-level \textbf{Precision}, \textbf{Recall}, and \textbf{F1} on the test set of each target domain, using exact span match. This evaluates whether the multi-view DTrans detector transfers better than the OWNER BIO-only baseline.

\paragraph{Entity Typing.}
We report \textbf{Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI)}~\cite{vinh-etal-2010-ami} to measure clustering quality. AMI is a mapping-free metric: it does not require any cluster-to-type assignment and is robust to permutations of cluster labels, over-segmentation, and over-merging. Scores close to 0 indicate random clustering; scores close to 1 indicate near-perfect agreement with the gold partition. We follow the OWNER paper~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} in using AMI as the primary entity typing metric.

\paragraph{Cluster Naming.}
We evaluate predicted cluster names against gold entity type names using \textbf{BERTScore-F1}~\cite{zhang-etal-2020-bertscore}, which measures semantic similarity between predicted and reference names using contextual embeddings.
Unlike exact-match accuracy, BERTScore-F1 awards partial credit for semantically close labels (e.g.\ \textit{researcher} vs.\ \textit{author}) and penalises completely wrong predictions, making it well-suited for evaluating open-vocabulary cluster names.
Figure~\ref{fig:bertscore_bar} illustrates the score range: exact copies score 1.0, synonym paraphrases score $\approx 0.95$, and completely wrong labels score $\approx 0.86$ with RoBERTa-large embeddings.

\section{Baselines and Ablations}

We compare against the following systems:

\paragraph{OWNER (baseline).}
The original OWNER pipeline~\cite{wang-etal-2023-crossner} uses a DeBERTa-v3-base BIO mention detector trained on the source domain, BERT fill-in-the-blank entity embeddings, BIC-guided $k$-means clustering, and single-template MLM naming without exemplar selection.

\paragraph{Span2Type-BIO.}
Replaces the DTrans multi-view detector with a single BIO head (same BERT backbone), keeping all other components identical to Span2Type-Full. This ablation isolates the contribution of multi-view detection.

\paragraph{Span2Type-SoftPrompt.}
Replaces the full BERT fine-tuning in the entity typing stage with the soft prompt variant (frozen PLM, $N_p = 5$ trainable tokens). All other components are identical to Span2Type-Full.

\paragraph{Span2Type-CentroidNaming.}
Uses the centroid-nearest single exemplar for cluster naming instead of MMR-based diverse selection. This ablation isolates the contribution of exemplar diversity.

\paragraph{Span2Type-Full.}
The complete system: DTrans multi-view entity detection + full BERT entity typing + BIC $k$-means + MMR-based MLM naming.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Effect of multi-view entity detection.}
Table~\ref{tab:mention_detection} shows that the DTrans multi-view detector (BIO-SE-TB) achieves an average F1 of 82.35 across five CrossNER domains, compared to 75.68 for the OWNER BIO-only baseline---a gain of 6.67 points. The improvement is largest in AI (+20.85) and smallest in Literature, where the BIO baseline remains competitive (83.9 vs 82.68). This suggests that the complementary SE and TB decoders are most beneficial in domains with irregular entity boundaries and diverse surface forms, while sequential BIO labeling suffices for more structured literary text.

\paragraph{End-to-end NER improvement.}
The main results in Table~\ref{tab:ner_comparison} show that Span2Type (CoNLL) achieves 61.4 average F1, a 12.0-point improvement over OWNER (49.4). This gap is substantially larger than the 6.67-point improvement at the entity detection stage alone, suggesting that the better span candidates from DTrans detection also improve downstream entity typing quality by reducing noise in the embeddings used for clustering.

\paragraph{Effect of source corpus.}
Span2Type (Pile-NER) achieves 55.42 average F1, lower than the CoNLL-source variant (61.4). The CrossNER domains are stylistically closer to CoNLL-2003 newswire than to the heterogeneous Pile-NER distribution, so the more focused CoNLL source produces a better-calibrated detector for these benchmarks.

\paragraph{Soft prompt tuning and ensemble detection.}
The ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_study} confirms that both the multi-view detector (+9.2 from 49.4 to 58.6) and the soft prompt entity typing (+2.8 from 58.6 to 61.4) contribute independently. The soft prompt variant reduces trainable parameters from $\sim$110M to $\sim$3,840 while still improving over the full fine-tuning OWNER baseline, demonstrating a favorable efficiency--performance trade-off.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{sec:Con}

This thesis presented \textbf{Span2Type}, an improved open-world NER pipeline built on the OWNER framework, with three targeted enhancements targeting the two weakest points of the original system: span detection quality and automatic entity type naming.

\section{Summary of Contributions}

We introduced three improvements to the OWNER pipeline and evaluated them systematically on the CrossNER benchmark across five target domains.

\textbf{First}, we replaced OWNER's single BIO mention detector with the DTrans multi-view Entity Detection module~\cite{zhang-etal-2024-dtrans}, which jointly trains BIO, Start-End, and Tie-Break decoders on the source domain with a Mean Teaching strategy. This change alone improved span detection F1 from an average of 75.68 (OWNER BIO) to 82.35 (BIO-SE-TB), a gain of 6.67 points across five CrossNER domains.

\textbf{Second}, we introduced a two-stage prompt-initialized fine-tuning approach for the entity typing stage. Stage~1 optimizes only $N_p = 5$ soft token embeddings ($\sim$3,840 parameters) while the BERT backbone is frozen, using a high learning rate ($3\times10^{-3}$) for rapid convergence. At epoch $E_{\text{unfreeze}} = 10$, Stage~2 unfreezes the backbone and continues training at a much lower learning rate ($1\times10^{-5}$) to prevent catastrophic forgetting, with the optimizer's accumulated moment estimates for the soft prompt preserved via \texttt{add\_param\_group()}. This two-stage strategy achieves parameter efficiency in early training and full-model expressiveness in later epochs, contributing a +2.8-point gain in end-to-end NER F1.

\textbf{Third}, we proposed an MMR-based exemplar selection strategy for automatic cluster naming. Rather than querying a naming model with the single centroid-nearest example per cluster, we use Maximal Marginal Relevance~\cite{carbonell-goldstein-1998-use} to select $K_e = 5$ diverse, centroid-relevant exemplars before passing them to either an MLM or a locally hosted LLM. This ensures the naming model receives evidence spanning the full semantic range of the cluster, producing more specific and stable type names.

On the CrossNER benchmark, Span2Type (CoNLL source) achieves an average end-to-end NER F1 of \textbf{61.4}, compared to \textbf{49.4} for the OWNER baseline—a 12.0-point improvement. Ablation experiments confirm that the multi-view detector contributes the largest share of this gain (+9.2 points), while soft prompt tuning provides a complementary additional improvement (+2.8 points).

\section{Limitations}

Despite these improvements, Span2Type retains several limitations inherited from the OWNER framework.

The entity detector is still trained on a labeled source domain and transferred to target domains; in settings where the source and target domains are very distant (e.g., newswire to clinical text), transfer quality degrades. Future work could explore self-training or domain-adaptive pretraining to bridge larger domain gaps.

The BIC-guided $k$ selection works well when the true number of entity types falls within the search range $[k_{\min}, k_{\max}]$, but it can still misestimate $k$ for domains with highly imbalanced type distributions or very fine-grained entity inventories. A more principled nonparametric clustering approach could improve robustness here.

The cluster naming quality depends on the availability and quality of the MLM or LLM backend. Single-token MLM names are sometimes too generic (e.g., \textit{entity}, \textit{person}), while LLM-based names, though richer, require a locally hosted model that may not always be accessible. Improving the post-processing and filtering of generated names remains an open problem.

\section{Future Work}

Several directions extend the work presented in this thesis. First, the soft prompt tuning approach could be extended with adapter layers or LoRA-based fine-tuning to capture more expressive adaptation with a still-limited parameter budget. Second, the MMR exemplar selection strategy could be applied not only at naming time but also during the entity typing training phase, selecting diverse in-context examples for few-shot or retrieval-augmented typing. Third, the BIC-guided clustering could be replaced with a hierarchical or density-based method that produces a type hierarchy rather than a flat partition, enabling more nuanced ontology induction. Finally, Span2Type could be evaluated on additional benchmarks beyond CrossNER, particularly in biomedical and legal domains where the need for open-world entity type discovery is most acute.
